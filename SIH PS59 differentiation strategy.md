# Optimise the 70 days nobody else models

**Research date: 20 September 2026. Competitor states are point-in-time observations. Every "absent" below means "absent from the sources we enumerated", not "proved absent".**

Your proposed pipeline — Environmental Data → Cleaning → Common Grid → Sea-Ice Forecast → Iceberg Trajectory → Risk Map → A\* → ETA/Fuel/Risk → Dashboard — is, component for component, the median architecture of the SIH26059 field. Four of six video teams and nearly every substantive repository independently converged on it, and we verified genuine `heapq`-based A\* in **four independent competitor repositories** while finding **exactly one repository in the entire field that declares `torch`** ([125112056-art requirements.txt](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/125112056-art/antarctic-nav-system/master/requirements.txt)). Building that pipeline better will not differentiate you, because the parts are free: OpenDrift's `openberg` ships iceberg drift physics with melting and rollover, BAS PolarRoute ships MIT-licensed ice-aware Antarctic routing with pre-built meshes, IceNet ships downloadable U-Net sea-ice weights, and **Mishra et al. (2021) already published Dijkstra route optimisation between Bharati and Maitri** from PDEU and ISRO's Space Applications Centre ([*Polar Science* 30, 100696](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S1873965221000736)). The one thing no competitor was found doing is reading NCPOR's own charter: tender **NCPOR/14(102)/21** buys **115 days of which only ~34 are transit and ~70 are spent stationary discharging cargo** at a landing area "chosen by Master of the vessel(s) in consultation with the Leader of the Expedition" ([41-ISEA Tender](https://www.indianembassyrome.gov.in/docs/1624279190_1285_41%20ISEA%20Tender-Ice%20Class%20Vessel.PDF)). That single document re-specifies the objective function: not shortest safe path between two points, but probability of completing both discharges inside the charter window across candidate discharge sites. Everything else in this report — a real vessel performance model, forecast-value routing, honest Monte Carlo, Polar Code compliance outputs — is supporting evidence for that reframe, ranked by how hard it is for another team to copy.

### Differentiator 1 — The discharge-decision reframe *(rank 1: highest defensibility, highest demo value)*

**Differentiator.** Change the decision variable from *which path between A and B* to *which discharge site, in which window, with what probability of completing both discharges inside the charter*. The route becomes the sub-problem, not the product.

**Problem solved.** NCPOR's indicative schedule, from its own global charter tender, is a 115-day charter in which **transit is 16 + 8 + 10 = 34 days (~30%)** and **70 days (~61%) are spent stationary at two discharge sites**:

| Event | Date | Days |
|---|---|---|
| ETD Cape Town | 23-Dec-2021 | 8 |
| **ETA Larsemann Hills (Bharati)** | 08-Jan-2022 | **16 — transit leg** |
| **ETD Larsemann Hills** | 17-Feb-2022 | **40 — on station** |
| **ETA India Bay (Maitri)** | 25-Feb-2022 | **8 — inter-station leg** |
| **ETD India Bay** | 27-Mar-2022 | **30 — on station** |
| ETA Cape Town | 06-Apr-2022 | **10 — return transit** |
| **Total** | | **115** |

([NCPOR/14(102)/21, 41-ISEA Tender, Annexure-I](https://www.indianembassyrome.gov.in/docs/1624279190_1285_41%20ISEA%20Tender-Ice%20Class%20Vessel.PDF)) — and the 43rd expedition sailed **exactly on that template date**, departing Cape Town 23 December 2023 and crossing into Antarctic waters 3 January 2024 ([PIB PRID 1993769](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleaseIframePage.aspx?PRID=1993769)). **A system that optimises only the sailing leg is optimising the minority of the charter.**

Three facts make the discharge a genuine decision rather than a fixed point. First, the charter party requires the vessel to proceed to the point "closest to landing area **chosen by Master of the vessel(s) in consultation with the Leader of the Expedition** for overboard discharge of Charterer's cargo/equipment **on fast ice for haulage to the inland area**" — a named, joint, contractual decision. Second, **ASMA 6 states that at the Larsemann Hills "no anchorages or barge landings are designated"** because of variable ice, vessels anchor **~5 nautical miles offshore**, and **India hauls by tracked vehicle over fast ice until mid-December and by barge after ice melt** ([ASMA No. 6 Management Plan](https://www.env.go.jp/nature/nankyoku/kankyohogo/database/jyouyaku/asma/asma_pdf_en/ASMA06_en.pdf)). Third, and decisively, **it has already gone wrong in a documented, Indian, loss-of-cargo event**: in April 2012 a slab of shelf ice **approximately 2 km × 1 km adjoining the "Indian Barrier"** — the cargo unloading site **about 100 km north of Maitri** — broke away and drifted to sea **carrying Indian tank containers and Russian cargo and fuel**, fragmenting into about ten icebergs; RADARSAT analysis showed "no signature of presence of metallic objects", i.e. the cargo most likely sank. The same NCPOR item records that **"heavy sea ice accumulation had already disrupted operations — both Russian and Indian expedition vessels could not reach their respective barriers in February/March 2012"** ([NCPOR news/view/147](https://ncpor.res.in/news/view/147)).

**Why competitors may not have it.** Every observed competitor optimises a point-to-point route. POLARPATH's on-screen scenario is a vessel bound for Bharati at 12 knots; IAVNS targets "R/V Bharathi" with origin/destination inputs; SagarDrishti's wireframe voyage planner takes departure port and destination port; the entire field's UI vocabulary is origin–destination. **We did not find a publicly accessible PS59 project, or any public system, that formulates the Antarctic resupply problem as discharge-site selection under a charter window.** Commercial systems behind sales walls cannot be ruled out. The reason is mundane and is your advantage: **the tender is not on the first page of a search for "Antarctic route planning"**, and no competitor's materials show any sign of having read it.

**Technical implementation.** A candidate discharge site is a point on the fast-ice or shelf edge inside the tender's own operating box (**66°–70°S, 80°–06°E, Prydz Bay/Larsemann Hills and India Bay**). For each candidate site *s* and each arrival window [t₀, t₁], compute five quantities: (a) **reachability** — does a POLARIS-feasible, resistance-feasible route to *s* exist in the forecast ice field; (b) **bearing capacity proxy** — is the ice at *s* fast ice of sufficient concentration/thickness persistence for tracked-vehicle haulage, versus barge-mode after melt; (c) **haul distance** from *s* to the station, which is a hard operational cost (100 km at Maitri's Indian Barrier is not a rounding error); (d) **iceberg and calving exposure** at *s* over the dwell window, from your drift model plus the ice-edge persistence field; (e) **mode**: tracked-vehicle-over-fast-ice versus barge, the binary ASMA 6 records with a mid-December transition. Then score each (site, window) pair by Monte Carlo over the forecast ensemble: **P(both discharges complete within the charter window)**. Present it as a small ranked table plus a map of candidate sites, not as a single route.

**Data required.** OSI SAF/Copernicus SIC and ice edge (free, daily, both hemispheres); a thickness field (any of the flawed ones, with its error stated); SCAR Antarctic Digital Database coastline and ice-shelf vectors ([SCAR ADD](https://scar.org/library-data/maps/add-digital-database)); AADC/CCAMLR ASPA/ASMA polygons ([data.gov.au AAD Protected/HSM GIS](https://www.data.gov.au/data/dataset/aad-protected-hsm-gis), [CCAMLR](https://www.ccamlr.org/en/data/aspas-and-asmas)); USNIC iceberg table; station coordinates (**Bharati 69°24.41'S, 76°11.72'E**, from ASMA 6). Nothing paid, nothing exotic.

**Difficulty: Medium.** The modelling is simple; the framing work is the hard part. There is no new algorithm here — the search you already have is reused, once per candidate site.

**Time required: 2–3 days**, most of it defining candidate sites and the scoring function, given a working router.

**Demo value: Very high.** The demo is: "here is the 2012 calving that took Indian containers to sea; here are four candidate discharge sites for this season; here is the probability each is reachable and still there in ten days; here is the one we recommend and the route falls out of it." That is a story no competitor's demo can tell, and it names a real Indian failure.

**Defensibility.** **High.** Another team cannot reach this by adding an ML model, because it is not a model — it is an objective function derived from a procurement document. Copying it requires reading NCPOR/14(102)/21, ASMA 6 and the 2012 NCPOR news item and then re-architecting. Within the SIH timeline that is not a copy, it is a rebuild.

**Evidence.** The 34/70-day split, the Master-plus-Expedition-Leader clause, the barge/crane/KA-32 discharge architecture and the 500 m fuel-hose-to-ice-shelf requirement are all verbatim in the tender; the "no designated anchorages / ~5 nm offshore / tracked vehicle until mid-December" facts are verbatim in ASMA 6; the 2012 cargo loss is NCPOR's own news item. Against that, **no competitor artifact in the roster mentions discharge, fast-ice bearing capacity, barge operations or the charter window at all.**

**The honest caveat to state.** The vessel also carries third-party cargo — **Princess Elizabeth Station (BELARE), Progress (Russian) and Novo Airport** ([PIB PRID 1993769](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleaseIframePage.aspx?PRID=1993769)) — so NCPOR does not have sole authority over the itinerary. Present the system as **advisory within a fixed multi-stop sequence**, not free re-planning. And **do not invent a charter day rate**: no awarded contract value, day-hire or demurrage rate for any ISEA charter was found in the public record. The tender gives the *formula* (day hire × days + victualling for 40 persons × days + mob/demob), so express savings as "**N days of day-hire plus victualling for 40 persons**" and let NCPOR substitute its own confidential rate. That is more credible than a fabricated rupee figure and it demonstrates you read their contract.

### Differentiator 2 — A vessel performance model with a published derivation *(rank 2)*

**Differentiator.** Replace the invented ice-difficulty penalty with a concentration-native ice-resistance law, a closed-form speed-in-ice inversion, a published fuel polynomial and IMO POLARIS — every term traceable to a named source, with stated error bars.

**Problem solved.** Every fuel and ETA number in the observed field is an assertion. IAVNS claims "**⛽ 8–15% Fuel Reduction**" and "6–14 hours saved" with no derivation; POLARPATH displays "fuel 45.5" from a service whose only non-stdlib import is `math`. Independent PS-specific analysis flags exactly this: *"Ice-aware routing needs a cost model for how hard a vessel finds a given ice concentration, and **without a vessel ice class and resistance model your route optimises a difficulty you invented**"* ([SIH Buddy SIH26059](https://www.sihbuddy.in/ps/SIH26059)).

**Why competitors may not have it.** Not because it is hard — because they do not know the formulation exists. BAS ships it in readable Python. The equations are **in the source, not in the docs**, so anyone who reads the PolarRoute documentation pages and stops will not find them.

**Technical implementation.** Port four functions from PolarRoute's [`SDA.py`](https://github.com/bas-logist/PolarRoute) — roughly 60 lines total:

- **Ice resistance, concentration-native:** `R_ice = 0.5 · k · Fr^b · ρ_ice · B · h · V² · C^n`, with `Fr = V / √(g·C·h)`. Hull parameters from source: **slender k = 4.4, b = −0.8267, n = 2.0; blunt k = 16.1, b = −1.7937, n = 3**.
- **Speed in ice:** if `C = 0`, run at max speed; if `C > max_ice_conc`, the cell is **inaccessible**; otherwise, if `R_ice` at max speed exceeds the force limit, **invert the law in closed form** — `vexp = 2·force_limit / (k·ρ·B·h·(C)ⁿ·(g·h·C)^(−b/2))`, `v = vexp^(1/(2+b))`.
- **Fuel, tonnes/day:** `24 · (1.37247e-3·V² − 2.9601e-3·V + 0.25290433 + 7.75218178e-11·R² + 6.48113363e-6·R)`, V in km/h, R in newtons. Note it is **additive-separable**, a calm-water speed term plus a resistance term — not a product.
- **Wave resistance (Kreitner, ITTC-recommended for small wave heights):** `R_wave = 0.64 · ρ_w · C_B · H_w² · B² / L`, valid to ~2 m.

The reference PC5 configuration is **max speed 26.5 km/h (~14.3 kn), beam 24.0 m, force limit 96,634.5 N, hard cut-off at 80% SIC, minimum depth 10 m**. Two free sanity checks come with it: at V = 0, R = 0 the polynomial gives **~6.07 t/day**, a plausible hotel load for a 15,000 t research vessel; at the force limit it gives **~32.4 t/day**. Layer POLARIS on top: `RIO = Σ Cᵢ × RIVᵢ`, C in tenths, **RIO ≥ 0 normal, −10 ≤ RIO < 0 elevated risk, RIO < −10 special consideration**, with Table 1.2 elevated-risk speed caps (**PC1 11 kn, PC2 8 kn, PC3–PC5 5 kn, below PC5 3 kn**) ([MSC.1/Circ.1519](https://www.nautinst.org/static/uploaded/2f01665c-04f7-4488-802552e5b5db62d9.pdf)).

**The India-specific twist that makes this more than a port.** PolarRoute's SDA is **PC5**. POLARPATH's on-screen vessel is **PC5**. But **NCPOR's tender requires ice class "equivalent to 1-A-super or better having double skinned hull"** — and POLARIS Table 1.3 has a separate **IA Super** row that is materially harsher. Our arithmetic on the verified table: in **Thick First-Year Ice**, PC5 carries RIV = 0 while IA Super carries RIV = −2, so at 10/10 concentration a PC5 scores RIO = 0 (**normal operation**) where an IA Super scores RIO = −20 (**operation subject to special consideration**). **Running POLARIS at the ice class NCPOR actually contracts for produces a visibly different accessible corridor from the PC5 everyone else assumes.** That is a ten-second, on-screen, India-specific result derived from two public documents.

**Data required.** SIC (free); a thickness field; wave height from ERA5; the POLARIS RIV table (a 12 × 12 array of integers, published); vessel beam and force limit (BAS's published PC5 config, substituted or scaled).

**Difficulty: Easy (Tier 1) to Medium (Tier 2).** Tier 1 is ~150 lines of Python plus a lookup table.

**Time required: 1–2 days for Tier 1; +2–3 days for Tier 2** (two independent resistance formulations shown side by side, thickness sensitivity at h × 0.5 and h × 1.5, ice-class sensitivity across PC5/PC7/IA Super).

**Demo value: High.** The strongest single moment is routing the *same* origin–destination pair twice — once with a naive concentration penalty of the kind the field uses, once with the physical model — and quantifying how the routes and fuel figures diverge.

**Defensibility. Medium-high.** Another team could port the same file in an afternoon **if they knew it existed** — but the port is not the defensible part. The defensible part is the honesty layer, which is harder to fake: **absolute fuel accuracy ±20–30%, relative route ranking considerably better**, benchmarked against a published full-scale comparison (ASPM predicted **870 kg/h** against a measured **790 kg/h** over a 100 km leg, ~10% over-prediction — [ASPM manuscript](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10137042/1/ASPM%20-%20final%20manuscript.pdf)) and against empirical ice-resistance errors of **0.4–9.6% ahead and 1.4–26.6% astern** for Lindqvist versus ice-model tests ([Fan et al. 2019](https://library.arcticportal.org/2707/1/A1904007.pdf)).

**Evidence.** Fuel claims in the field carry no derivation; the risk map's only code-verified weights are invented; no competitor names an ice class *and* derives a resistance model from it.

**The honest caveats you must state, because they are what make the claim survive questioning.**

| Caveat | The statement to make |

### Differentiator 3 — Forecast-value routing: the three-run protocol *(rank 3: highest intellectual defensibility, lowest demo flash)*

**Differentiator.** Stop reporting forecast accuracy and start reporting **forecast value**: plan a route on your forecast, score it on the ice that actually happened, and compare against planning on persistence. The difference is, in decision units, what your model is worth.

**Problem solved.** MAE does not tell an operator anything. A forecast can have better MAE than persistence and produce identical routing decisions — in which case it is worth nothing operationally. Conversely a forecast with mediocre MAE can shift a route away from a corridor that closed. **The reviewed routing literature does not run this experiment**: an Arctic weather-routing review records that experiments "use actual historical data from November 2020 but **treat sea ice data as if perfectly known**", representing "a hindcast scenario rather than forward forecasting with uncertainty quantification", and notes the absence of any discussion of "forecast error propagation through routing decisions" ([*Frontiers in Marine Science* 2023](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/marine-science/articles/10.3389/fmars.2023.1190164/full)).

**The protocol.** Three route computations per origin–destination pair per initialisation date:

| Run | Planned on | Scored on | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A. Perfect-prognosis upper bound** | Observed/analysis fields at t+1…t+n | The same observed fields | The best your router could ever do. Not achievable operationally. |
| **B. Operational** | **Your forecast** initialised at t | Observed fields at t+1…t+n | **The honest number.** |
| **C. Persistence-planned** | Today's ice frozen forward | Observed fields at t+1…t+n | The no-model baseline router. |

Then two gaps: **value of the forecast = cost(C) − cost(B)** — *"our forecast is worth N hours of transit and X ice-hours per voyage versus planning on persistence"* — and **cost of forecast error = cost(B) − cost(A)**. Report alongside them the **failure rate**: the fraction of voyages where the route planned on the forecast, scored on the observed field, violates a hard constraint (enters SIC above the ice class limit, or RIO < −10). A route that is cheap on average and violates a constraint 8% of the time is not deployable, and reporting that number is what separates an engineering team from a demo team.

**Why competitors may not have it.** Two independent teams publish a baseline comparison at all. **Neither runs a route on a forecast and scores it on observation.** The PS-specific third-party analysis recommends precisely the adjacent experiment — *"Show a forecast ice field for a date you held out, overlay the actual observed ice for that date to prove the forecast held, and then run the router to a station"* ([SIH Buddy](https://www.sihbuddy.in/ps/SIH26059)) — and **zero observed teams do it**.

**Technical implementation.** You already have the router. Run it three times per date with different cost fields and score all three polylines against the same observed field. It is a loop, not a new algorithm. The elegant property: **run C also tests whether the forecast model is worth building at all, independent of its MAE.** If cost(C) ≈ cost(B), your forecast adds no decision value — a genuinely interesting negative result and a strong thing to publish.

**Data required.** A held-out set of initialisation dates with matching observations. Nothing else.

**Difficulty: Easy-Medium.** **Time required: 1 day** given a working router and a forecast.

**Demo value: Medium.** It is a table, not a visual. Lead the demo with the map; hold this for the technical Q&A, where it is decisive.

**Defensibility. High.** Adding an ML model does not reproduce it; it is an experimental design, and it is the design that a domain judge from an earth-science ministry will recognise immediately as the right one.

**Evidence.** Zero of six video teams report any skill number against any baseline; the routing literature itself does not run this experiment; the only two committed real-data baseline comparisons in the field both show a loss at short lead.

**Pair it with the masking argument, which is the cheapest rigour available.** A competitor's own README states its **MAE is 0.003 over open water and 0.097 in the marginal ice zone, and that 97% of the domain is open water**, noting "a single overall MAE of 0.019 hides that entirely". Their domain-wide figure is therefore roughly 94% determined by trivially predictable cells. The literature's answer is to mask: **IceNet zero-weights everything outside a dynamically-sized "active grid cell region" and normalises its headline metric by that region — "Binary accuracy = (1 − IIEE / area of active grid cell region) × 100%"** ([Nature Communications](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-25257-4)). Report on four masks side by side — full domain (to show it is uninformative), active region, marginal ice zone (0.15 ≤ SIC < 0.80), and an ice-edge band — and **mask on the observation, never on the forecast**, because masking on the forecast lets the model choose its own exam. Lead with **length-normalised IIEE in kilometres**, `D_IIEE_AVG = 2·A_IIEE / (L_O + L_M)` ([Melsom et al., *Ocean Science* 15, 615](https://os.copernicus.org/articles/15/615/2019/)), because "our ice edge is on average X km out of place, persistence is Y km out of place" is legible to anyone in the room. For the significance claim, use a **paired moving-block bootstrap on the per-initialisation score difference** — block length ≈ the decorrelation time of the difference series, justified from its autocorrelation function — and report the **effective sample size**, not the nominal one: with n = 300 daily initialisations and a 20-day block, you have roughly **15 independent blocks**, which supports a per-lead comparison and does not support a seasonal or cross-sector generalisation claim.

### Differentiator 4 — Monte Carlo route re-scoring under spatially correlated perturbations *(rank 4)*

**Differentiator.** Score a **fixed** route across N perturbed forecast fields and report a distribution — **ETA P10/P50/P90, P(route blocked), worst-decile ETA** — instead of a single number.

**Problem solved.** Every route figure in the field is a point estimate with no spread. A competitor showing "22.6 h" cannot answer "how sure are you?" You can.

**Why competitors may not have it — and the novelty limit you must respect.** In the observed field, uncertainty appears as rhetoric (MitoNaut, with no numbers across 15:29), as a hardcoded dictionary (POLARPATH), or as an untrained module (`uncertainty.py` with no weights). **But this is not novel in the literature and you must not claim it is.** "**Robust Pareto-optimum routing of ships utilising deterministic and ensemble weather forecasts**" already exists as a published idea ([record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/232940270_Robust_Pareto-optimum_routing_of_ships_utilising_deterministic_and_ensemble_weather_forecasts)) — we could not obtain its full text, so **read it before claiming anything is new**. The analytical analogue is published too: a JMSE paper propagates ensemble means and standard deviations through First-Order Second-Moment reliability analysis and computes probability of constraint violation as **`p(g ≤ 0) = Φ(−μ_g / σ_g)`**, plotting 90% confidence bands on fuel and speed ([JMSE 9(12):1434](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1312/9/12/1434)). **The claimable version is: "a standard robustness evaluation, which we implement and surface in the UI, and which hackathon-scale systems omit."**

**Technical implementation, and the one detail that makes it credible.** Plan once; re-score N times. Re-scoring a fixed polyline is O(waypoints) — 200 waypoints × 200 members is 40,000 field lookups, milliseconds in NumPy. **The expensive thing is re-planning per member; do only the cheap thing.** The critical detail: **the perturbation must be a spatially correlated random field with a correlation length of order 100–200 km, not per-cell iid noise.** Independent per-cell noise averages out along the route and produces an absurdly tight ETA distribution — the classic tell of a naive Monte Carlo. Two lines: `gaussian_filter(np.random.randn(H, W), sigma=corr_cells)`, renormalised to unit variance, then `field = mu + sigma * grf`, clipped to [0, 1]. **CVaR comes free** from the same sample — `mean(sorted(etas)[-int(0.1*N):])` displayed as "worst-decile ETA" — but say "**we report CVaR**", never "we optimise CVaR"; optimising it over paths is the hard research problem.

**Data required.** Your own forecast plus a spread field — real ensemble members if the product has them, otherwise a stated σ field.

**Difficulty: Easy. Time required: half a day to 1 day.** This is the best value-per-hour item in the entire brief.

**Demo value: High.** An ETA fan instead of a number; "P(blocked by impassable ice) = 8%".

**Defensibility. Low-medium — and be honest about that.** Another team could add it in an afternoon. What they will get wrong is the correlated field and the spurious precision. **Never show a confidence to one decimal place unless N ≥ 1000** — a 200-sample Monte Carlo has ~±3% binomial standard error at p = 0.9, so show "92% (±3%)" or bucket into High/Medium/Low. And **confidence must fall as the voyage extends past the forecast horizon**; if the slider moves and confidence never changes, a judge will correctly assume it is hardcoded.

**Evidence.** The uncertainty column of the competitor matrix is the emptiest one; nobody publishes a calibration curve, a coverage number or a probabilistic score.

## PART 4 — Decision support under uncertainty

### The four route archetypes and their metrics

| Archetype | Objective | Per-route metrics to show |
|---|---|---|
| **Safety-conservative** | Minimise POLARIS deficit and iceberg encounter probability | POLARIS deficit (RIO-hours), min CPA to nearest tracked berg (nm), P(any encounter), hours in SIC > 0.6, max SIC |
| **Fuel-efficient** | Minimise fuel tonnes from the resistance model | Fuel (t) with ±20–30% band, mean/max SIC traversed, distance (nm) |
| **Fastest** | Minimise transit time under the speed-in-ice inversion | ETA P10/P50/P90, worst-decile ETA, distance |
| **Balanced** | Weighted scalarisation, user-adjustable | All of the above plus P(blocked), confidence with its driver named |

**Sea-ice exposure should be time-weighted, not distance-weighted**, because a ship crawling at 4 kn through 8/10 ice is far more exposed than one crossing the same distance at 14 kn: `IceExposure = Σ SIC(cell, τ_arrival) · Δt_segment` (ice-hours), with **`HoursAboveThresh` as the headline** — a navigator understands "9.4 h in ≥6/10 ice" — and **`POLARIS_deficit = Σ Δt · max(0, −RIO)`** as the most defensible single risk scalar available, because it is anchored to an IMO methodology and to a specific ice class rather than to an invented weighting. For icebergs, do not use closest approach alone: widen an error cone with lead time, `σ_b(t) = √(σ₀² + (v_err·t)²)`, compute `p_encounter = exp(−d²/(2σ_b²))` at closest approach, and combine as `P_any = 1 − Π(1 − p_b)` — **stating openly that independence across bergs is an approximation, since bergs in one drift stream share current error**. These are *your operationalisations*, not standard metrics: **no source gives an agreed formulation for "sea-ice exposure along a route" or "iceberg exposure"**, so present them as such.

| Grid A\* with configurable scalarised cost; weight sweep + Pareto filter; penalty-method diversification; per-route metrics; risk-segment max-subarray; Monte Carlo re-scoring with a correlated field; ETA percentiles | ~1 day each | **Build** |

### What competitors already do here — be precise, because the precision is the weapon

**POLARPATH AI (Team AXIOM)** claims, on video, "**A\*, D\* Lite along with Pareto**", "generates Pareto-optimal multi-objective navigation routes (Safety, Fuel, Time)", "quantifies forecast uncertainty", and shows on-screen route comparison with a risk reduction from ~86.4% to ~25% ([video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4W7-kpP6dHQ)). Its [`route_optimizer.py`](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/afrazhussaina5/polarpath-ai/main/backend/services/route_optimizer.py) is 14,435 bytes importing only `math` and schemas: **there is no `heapq`, no `dijkstra`, no `a_star` and no priority queue anywhere in the file.** Routing is five hand-written builders — `_build_route_alpha`, `_build_route_beta_safety`, `_build_route_gamma_speed`, `_build_route_charlie_evasion`, `_build_route_delta_deep_detour` — selected by an `if priority == "safety" / elif priority in ["time","speed"]` ladder, with the branch key literally `has_hazard = "IB-042" in iceberg_service.icebergs`. **IAVNS (Team BYTE DRAGON)** by contrast has a verified 24,873-byte `route_service.py` opening `"""A* router on the shared analysis grid.` with `import heapq` and a real `heapq.heappush(open_set, (0.0, start_idx))`, offering **FASTEST / SAFEST / BALANCED / CUSTOM** with user sliders and an explainable risk breakdown panel.

**The implication for you.** "We show four routes and a slider" is *already shipped* by at least one competitor with real code behind it, and *claimed* by several more. **Do not differentiate on the feature. Differentiate on what is verifiably computed and on what you refuse to claim.** The single cheapest way to do that on stage: **show the node-expansion count and re-solve latency** — "re-solved in 180 ms, 4,812 nodes expanded". It is cheap, impossible to fake convincingly, and it directly answers the question a judge who has read one of these repos will be asking.

### Two rigour traps that will bite you

**Trap 1 — path standard deviation is not additive.** If you set per-edge cost `c_e = μ_e + λ·σ_e` and run A\*, you minimise `Σμ_e + λ·Σσ_e`. The true mean-risk objective is **`μᵀx + c·√(τᵀx)`** ([Lim, Sommer, Nikolova & Rus, RSS 2012](https://www.roboticsproceedings.org/rss08/p32.pdf)), whose risk term is non-additive — which is exactly why the mean-risk shortest path is a hard combinatorial problem rather than plain Dijkstra. Since `√(Σσ²) ≤ Σσ` for independent edges, **the additive form systematically over-penalises long routes through uncertain water**. The good news is that it is defensible here: sea-ice and weather forecast errors are spatially correlated over scales much larger than a grid cell, so "perfectly correlated along the route" is the more appropriate end of the range. **But the assumption must be stated.** The sentence: *"We use an additive risk-sensitive edge cost `c = mean + λ·std`. This is the standard mean-variance surrogate; it equals the true path-level objective under the assumption of perfectly correlated forecast error along the route, and is an upper bound otherwise. We do not claim to solve the exact mean-risk shortest path problem, which is non-additive and combinatorially hard."*

**Trap 2 — a variance penalty does not always cause detours, and can silently be a no-op.** The behaviour depends entirely on the σ field:

- **Detours appear** when σ is spatially heterogeneous **and not collinear with μ** — a corridor with low mean ice but high ensemble disagreement gets avoided as λ rises. The marginal ice zone is physically exactly where this should happen, because ice-edge position is where spread is largest. **This is the demo behaviour you want.**
- **No detour** when σ is roughly uniform: `∫λσ ds ≈ λ·σ̄·L`, a constant times path length, so raising λ just makes the route *straighter*. **A demo built on a synthetic uniform-uncertainty field shows nothing.**
- **No new behaviour at all** when **σ ∝ μ** — which is exactly what happens if you derive "uncertainty" from the magnitude of the ice field rather than from real ensemble spread. Then `μ + λσ = μ(1 + λα)`, a pure rescale, and **the argmin path is identical for every λ. The slider is silently a no-op.** This is the most likely way a student team accidentally ships a broken control. **Write the unit test: `assert route(λ=0) != route(λ=2)` on the real field.**
- **Perverse case:** a short route crossing a small high-σ patch can lose to a long detour, because the additive form charges λσ on *every* cell including calm ones. Fix with `μ_e + λ·max(0, σ_e − σ_floor)` so background uncertainty does not tax route length.

Ship an **uncertainty-field layer toggle** alongside the slider. When the route visibly bends around a bright patch, the causal story is legible in two seconds; without the layer the route just moves and nobody knows why.

### Say "non-dominated candidates", never "the Pareto front"

Sweeping weights over the simplex and minimising `w₁·time + w₂·fuel + w₃·risk` recovers only the **supported** efficient solutions — those on the convex hull of the achievable objective set. de Weck & Kim state it flatly of the classical weighted-sum method: *"more seriously, **optimal solutions in non-convex regions are not detected**"*, and note the second failure mode that matters for a demo, that *"often the optimal solution distribution is not uniform"* — an evenly spaced weight sweep produces **clustered**, not evenly spaced, solutions ([de Weck & Kim, MIT](https://web.mit.edu/deweck/www/PDF_archive/2%20Refereed%20Journal/2_12_SMO_AWSMOO1_deWeck_Kim.pdf)). On a discrete graph this is not a corner case: points strictly inside the convex hull of a finite objective cloud are routinely Pareto-optimal yet minimise no linear scalarisation.

**The honest wording:** *"We compute a set of **non-dominated candidate routes** by scalarised A\* over a sweep of preference weights, then filter by Pareto dominance. This recovers the **supported** (convex-hull) efficient solutions; routes in non-convex regions of the true efficient frontier are not guaranteed to be found."* Label the plot panel **"Trade-off view (supported solutions)"**. Never "Pareto-optimal routing" or "exact Pareto". **One sentence acknowledging this limitation is a credibility asset** — especially standing next to a competitor claiming "Pareto-optimal multi-objective routes" from a file with no priority queue in it.

**The addition that reaches what the sweep cannot** is the **epsilon-constraint / risk-budget mode**: minimise time subject to risk ≤ B, approximated cheaply by running A\* on time with hard cell exclusion where `risk > B`, sweeping B. It reaches non-convex regions of the front, and it maps perfectly onto navigator language — *"I will not accept more than X hours above SIC 0.6."* Describe it as **"risk-budget masking"**, not "exact constrained shortest path": exact resource-constrained shortest path is NP-hard in general and the thresholded mask is an approximation.

### Make the alternatives visually distinct, then measure it

Three overlapping lines read as a rendering bug. Use both mechanisms. **Generation-side, the penalty method:** after accepting a route, multiply the cost of its edges — and of edges in a spatial tube around it — by a factor, then re-search; the literature-reported factor range is **k ∈ [1..2]** with a "rejoin penalty" and a distance-decaying tube ([alternative-routes literature](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1002.4330); the safe citation for the concept is [Improved Alternative Route Planning, ATMOS 2013](https://drops.dagstuhl.de/storage/01oasics/oasics-vol033-atmos2013/OASIcs.ATMOS.2013.108/OASIcs.ATMOS.2013.108.pdf) — **the numeric attribution between two overlapping papers could not be cleanly separated, so re-check it before putting it on a slide**). Tune the tube radius to about 3–8% of the origin–destination great-circle distance so separations are visible at demo zoom. **Selection-side, an overlap constraint:** reject any candidate whose shared-edge fraction exceeds θ against an already-selected route, the k-SPwLO formulation ([Chondrogiannis et al.](https://pbour.github.io/docs/sigspatial15_poster.pdf)); measure overlap only on the middle 80% of the route so unavoidable shared approaches do not dominate. Then **report the separation number** — "max lateral separation: 84 nm" — which converts a visual property into a defensible metric and pre-empts "are these actually different?"

### One more honest statement that almost no team will know to make

If you make A\*'s state `(cell, arrival_time)`, you are relying on **FIFO**: *"for each pair of time instants τ, τ′ with τ < τ′ … c(u,v,τ) + τ ≤ c(u,v,τ′) + τ′"*, and the standard result is that the shortest-path problem is polynomially solvable in FIFO time-dependent networks while **NP-hard in non-FIFO networks** ([Nannicini et al.](https://www.lix.polytechnique.fr/~liberti/bidirtimedepj.pdf)). For a ship, FIFO means *waiting never helps you arrive earlier* — **which is violated in real ice navigation**, where waiting 12 hours for a lead to open or a storm to pass genuinely can get you there sooner. Say so: *"We assume FIFO, which preserves A\*'s optimality on the time-expanded graph. This is violated in ice, where waiting for favourable drift can reduce transit time; modelling deliberate waiting needs explicit wait arcs, which we do not implement."*

### The yardstick warning — read this before you publish any 24-hour drift number

**The only retrievable operational figure is that an iceberg drift model validated against 17 observed drift tracks grows absolute position error at about 12 km per day**, with reductions "up to 35% at 96 hour forecast periods" relative to its predecessor ([TRID 1391690](https://trid.trb.org/view/1391690)). **The one-line diagnostic: run constant velocity on identical pairs. If it also scores near-zero, your targets are interpolated.** Linear interpolation between two weekly fixes produces a straight line at constant speed, and a constant-velocity baseline scores near-perfectly against it. ### Will judges reward this? The balanced answer

**There is no direct measured evidence on how judges react to self-critical results, and this report will not invent any.** What exists points in both directions and the asymmetry is the finding.

**Against.** Devpost's published guidance on judging criteria lists technical quality, user experience, demo quality, real-world value, innovation and design, and **does not mention honesty, testing evidence, documentation or validation at all** ([Devpost](https://info.devpost.com/blog/understanding-hackathon-submission-and-judging-criteria)). **For.** In the culture the problem statement comes from, this is table stakes. The NeurIPS Paper Checklist requires authors to state whether the paper "reports error bars suitably and correctly defined", encourages a separate Limitations section naming "strong assumptions and how robust the results are to violations", and requires code and instructions covering "your new method **and baselines**" ([NeurIPS](https://neurips.cc/public/guides/PaperChecklist)). The risk is real but it is a **risk of framing, not of content**, and the dominant strategy is non-exclusive: **validation is a second asset, not a substitute for a working demo. Build both; lead with the demo; hold the validation as the depth that survives questioning.** A generalist judge is unmoved by a confidence interval but immediately understands "Ours: 39 km. No-model baseline: 61 km." Reserve the bootstrap intervals for the repo and the technical Q&A.

### The five-slot framing formula

Every partial-skill statement must fill all five slots. This is induced from how the cited papers actually write, and it converts an admission into a demonstration of judgement.

1. **Reference named** — "against damped anomaly persistence with per-cell lag-τ damping fitted on training years only"
2. **Region scoped** — "in the marginal ice zone (0.15 ≤ SIC < 0.80), which is 3% of the domain and 100% of the navigational decisions"
3. **Lead time scoped** — "at lead times 3–7 days"
4. **Margin quantified with uncertainty** — "skill score 0.22, 95% paired block-bootstrap CI [0.11, 0.32], n = 300 initialisations, effective n ≈ 15 blocks"
5. **Failure named and explained** — "at lead 1 day persistence is better (SS = −0.07), as expected: day-to-day SIC autocorrelation is near 1 and no model should beat persistence there. **Our system therefore uses persistence at lead 1.**"

**Slot 5 is the whole trick.** Ship a **lead-time-dependent model selector** — persistence at short leads, the learned model in the middle band, climatology at long leads, with the crossover chosen on validation data. It is cheap to implement, it turns the entire negative result into a product feature, and **"we use the baseline where the baseline wins" is an engineering decision, not a failure.** Note that a competitor's committed evidence already sets the bar for what beating persistence at lead 1 costs: its own `baseline_comparison.json` records ice-zone MAE of **persistence 0.02051 versus model 0.04662** at +1 day (n = 300), with climatology at 0.11246, and the pattern holding at +3 days ([raw JSON](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/nawddeep/SIH26059/main/seaice_forecast/output/evaluation/baseline_comparison.json)). Another team's `EVALUATION.md` shows the same shape on drift: ADE at 1 day **3.86 km against a constant-velocity baseline's 3.70 km — a loss** — near-parity at 3 days, and a win at 7 days (22.65 against 24.91). **The honest pattern in this domain is that trivial baselines own the short horizon.** Design around it rather than pretending otherwise.

**The cheapest credibility win available:** publish a `splits.json` giving the exact initialisation dates of every held-out sample, plus the code that reproduces the baseline numbers. Fit normalisation statistics, climatology and damping coefficients on the training period only — **fitting climatology on the full record is leakage into the baseline, which paradoxically makes the baseline look unfairly strong, and is still wrong.** Evaluate model and baselines on **exactly the same mask, interpolation and grid**; a baseline evaluated on a different mask is not a baseline.

At Larsemann Hills there are no designated anchorages. In April 2012 the ice at the Indian Barrier calved and took Indian tank containers to sea. So our system does not answer 'what is the shortest safe path'. It answers 'which discharge site, in which window, gives the highest probability of completing both discharges inside the charter'. The route is the sub-problem."

**The 1-minute explanation.** Add three things. First, the vessel model: "our ice resistance, speed-in-ice inversion and fuel curve are the formulation the British Antarctic Survey uses to route the RRS *Sir David Attenborough*, ported from their open-source code and cited, plus IMO POLARIS from MSC.1/Circ.1519 — and we run POLARIS at **1A Super**, the ice class NCPOR's tender actually requires, not the PC5 everyone assumes. At thick first-year ice that is the difference between RIO 0 and RIO −20: normal operation versus special consideration. Absolute fuel is ±20–30%; relative route ranking is much better; we show the error bars." Second, the validation: "we report forecast skill against four references — climatology, persistence, anomaly persistence and damped anomaly persistence — masked to the marginal ice zone, with paired bootstrap intervals. We do not beat persistence at one-day lead, and we do not claim to; our system uses persistence there. Then we do the thing the routing literature does not: we plan routes on the forecast and score them on the ice that actually happened, so we can tell you what the forecast is *worth* in hours and ice-exposure, not just its MAE." Third, the uncertainty: "every route number is a distribution from re-scoring across 200 spatially correlated perturbed ice fields, and every route ships a Polar Code Chapter 11 evidence sheet and an Indian Antarctic Act discharge record."

**The three strongest differentiating points.** (1) **The objective function is NCPOR's, not the problem statement's paraphrase** — discharge-site selection under a charter window, grounded in tender NCPOR/14(102)/21, ASMA 6 and a documented 2012 Indian cargo loss. (2) **Every physical number has a published derivation and a stated error band**, against a field where fuel figures are assertions. (3) **Forecast value, not forecast accuracy** — the three-run protocol that the reviewed routing literature does not run and no observed competitor runs.

**What to say to judges.** Name PolarRoute and Mishra et al. *first, before they do*: "the routing physics is not our contribution — BAS and Mishra et al. from PDEU and ISRO SAC have done that, and Mishra's case study is literally Bharati to Maitri. We build on them and cite them." Say "this is the charterer's instrument, not the bridge's" — the Master is FESCO's and holds ice-piloting authority; your system produces evidence the Voyage Leader puts in front of the Master and NCPOR HQ. Express savings in NCPOR's own contractual units — **N days of day hire plus victualling for 40 persons** — and let them substitute their confidential rate. Quote the IMO against yourself: POLARIS "should not be interpreted as a 'Go/No Go' tool but as a decision support tool." And state the limitations before you are asked: FIFO is violated in ice; the weight sweep reaches supported solutions only; Antarctic thickness carries a ~38% model bias; your RIO is thickness-derived, not ice-chart-derived; you use Table 1.3, never Table 1.4.

**What NOT to claim.** Not "first AI polar navigation system" (PolarRoute is production, MIT-licensed, with pre-built Antarctic meshes). Not "first optimum route between Indian stations" (*Polar Science* 30, 100696, 2021 — **false and it will be caught**). Not "first deep-learning sea-ice forecast" (IceNet, *Nature Communications*, with downloadable weights). Not "we invented POLARIS integration" (it is a lookup table and a weighted sum from 2016, and ABS ships RIO contour maps). Not "nobody does iceberg-aware multi-objective routing" — and note that the specific "Zvyagina & Zvyagin 2022" and "Lee et al. 2021 POLARIS routing" citations **could not be re-confirmed in the second research pass**, so cite the POLARIS and ice-routing sources you can verify rather than those two. Not "Monte Carlo route robustness is novel" (robust Pareto ensemble ship routing is published). Not "no system does X" — the correct wording is **"we did not find a publicly accessible system that…"**, with an explicit note that commercial systems behind sales walls cannot be ruled out. Not a charter day rate, a cargo tonnage, or MV *Vasiliy Golovnin*'s Polar Class — **the tender's 1A-Super requirement is verified; the specific vessel's assigned class is not**, so present a class sensitivity range instead. Not "our model achieves 96% accuracy". Not any confidence number to one decimal place.

### "If 20 other teams are solving this exact PS, why should our solution look different when a judge sees the demo?"

**Because the first thing on screen is not a map.** It is NCPOR's own 115-day charter schedule with 70 days highlighted, and the April 2012 RADARSAT story of Indian tank containers going to sea from the Indian Barrier. Within fifteen seconds the judge knows this team read the sponsor's procurement documents and news archive, not just the 277-character problem statement — and every other demo that morning opened with a rotating globe.

**Because the demo answers a different question.** Every other submission will show an origin box, a destination box, and three coloured lines. Yours shows **four candidate discharge sites**, each with a probability of being reachable *and* still intact across the dwell window, and the route appears as a consequence of choosing one. A judge who has just watched four identical origin–destination demos will notice a different question being asked — and the question is the sponsor's own, because the charter names the Master and the Expedition Leader as the people who make exactly this choice.

**Because the numbers have provenance and the others do not.** When a judge asks where the fuel figure comes from, the answers available in this field are silence, "8–15%", or "45.5". Yours is a named resistance law with published coefficients, a closed-form speed inversion, a fuel polynomial that returns a plausible 6 t/day hotel load at zero speed, and an error band benchmarked against a full-scale comparison that over-predicted by ~10%. When the judge asks about risk weights, the field's answer is 0.35/0.30/0.20/0.15; yours is a POLARIS RIO computed at the ice class NCPOR's tender actually requires, with the corridor visibly shrinking when you switch from PC5 to 1A Super on screen.

**Because you volunteer the loss before you are asked.** Everyone else will show a high accuracy number. You will show a table where **persistence beats you at one-day lead**, immediately followed by a system that *uses* persistence at one-day lead, and then by the number nobody else has: what your forecast is worth in hours and ice-hours when you plan on it and score on what actually happened. In a room where a domain judge can puncture an unvalidated "96% accuracy" with a single question about how much of the domain is open water, **the team that already stratified its own metrics is the only one with an answer ready.** That is the difference between a demo that survives ten minutes of Q&A from an MoES panel and one that does not — and with only four or five teams advancing per problem statement from a sponsor that is explicitly "not obligated to declare a winner", surviving the Q&A is the whole game.
